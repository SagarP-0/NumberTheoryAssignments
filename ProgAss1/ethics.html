U1·Q1

Define ethics. Distinguish between morals, ethics, and laws with examples.

Definition of Ethics

Ethics is called a normative science because it establishes norms, standards, and principles to evaluate human behavior. It focuses on what "ought to be" rather than just describing "what is." Professional ethics (or codes of conduct) are specialized external standards that define appropriate behavior within a specific industry or organization. Personal ethics is an individual's internal, self-defined moral compass — e.g., honesty, integrity, and compassion. Ethics help maintain trust in society and organizations by ensuring actions are fair, transparent, and accountable.

Differences Between Morals, Ethics, and Laws

Aspect Morals Ethics Laws
Definition Personal internal principles about right and wrong External professional/group standards of conduct Official mandatory rules enforced by the state
Source Internal — self, family, religion, culture External — professional bodies, society, organizations Government / Legislature
Enforcement Conscience or social disapproval (shame) Professional consequences (e.g., losing a license) Courts, police, fines, imprisonment
Flexibility Can change with personal growth; flexible Formal and well-structured Strict; follows established policy
Focus What you believe is right, regardless of others Acceptable behavior in a specific role/context Maintaining social order
Example Refusing to cheat on an exam because it conflicts with personal values A lawyer defending a guilty client because legal ethics require vigorous defense Stopping at a red light — mandatory regardless of personal opinion

Moral vs Ethics Situation Example

A manager discovers that reporting a financial mistake will harm the company's reputation. Morally, the manager believes honesty is important. Ethically, company policy and professional accounting ethics require accurate reporting. If the manager hides the mistake, he violates both professional ethics and potentially his own moral values.

Key Takeaway: Morals are personal and internal; ethics are professional and external; laws are state-enforced and mandatory. A person can act ethically but immorally (e.g., defending a guilty client), or morally but illegally (e.g., speeding to save a life).
U1·Q2

Define Information System. List and explain its components.

Definition of Information System

An Information System (IS) is a system that collects, processes, stores, and distributes information. It combines people, hardware, software, data, and procedures to support decision-making and control in an organization.

Components of an Information System

  1. People: Users who operate and manage the system — including IT professionals, end users, managers, and stakeholders.
  2. Hardware: Physical devices such as computers, servers, storage devices, input/output devices, and networking equipment.
  3. Software: Programs and applications that process data — including operating systems, databases, and application software.
  4. Data: Raw facts collected, processed, and stored by the system. Data is transformed into useful information for decision-making.
  5. Procedures: Rules, policies, and methods that govern how data is collected, processed, stored, and distributed — both manual and automated.
  6. Network: Communication infrastructure that connects components and enables data transfer — including LANs, WANs, and the Internet.
All six components must work together effectively for an information system to function correctly and ethically.
U1·Q3

Explain the importance of ethics in Information Technology.

Ethics in Information Technology

Ethics in IT refers to the moral guidelines, principles, and standards governing the responsible development, usage, and management of technology and data.

Importance

  • Ensures responsible use of technology: Prevents misuse of computing resources, data, and networks for harmful purposes.
  • Builds trust with clients and society: Ethical IT practices foster confidence in digital systems, organizations, and professionals.
  • Reduces risk of cybercrime and data misuse: Ethical awareness prevents actions that lead to data breaches, hacking, and privacy violations.
  • Supports innovation while respecting rights: Ensures technological advancement does not violate intellectual property, human rights, or privacy.
  • Enhances corporate reputation: Organizations with strong ethical cultures attract better clients, talent, and investment.
  • Prevents legal and social issues: Ethical compliance prevents lawsuits, fines, and criminal charges. Example: GDPR compliance protects user privacy and avoids heavy penalties.

Core Principles for IT Professionals

  • Honesty & Transparency: Being clear about the purpose and impact of technology.
  • Integrity: Maintaining high standards of professional behavior.
  • Respect: Honoring users' privacy rights.
  • Accountability: Taking responsibility for the impact of technology solutions.

Key Aspects of IT Ethics

  • Privacy and Data Security: Protecting personal information from unauthorized collection, tracking, and data breaches.
  • Intellectual Property Rights: Respecting copyrights, patents, and trade secrets in software and hardware development.
  • Accountability: Taking responsibility for automated decisions and environmental impacts.
  • Fairness and Accessibility: Preventing bias in AI and ensuring technology does not discriminate or harm users.
U1·Q4

What is integrity in information systems? How is it maintained?

Definition of Integrity

Integrity in information systems is the foundational security principle that ensures data is accurate, consistent, and reliable throughout its lifecycle. It guarantees that information is not improperly modified, corrupted, or deleted by unauthorized users or system errors.

Key Aspects of Information System Integrity

  • Data Accuracy & Consistency: Ensuring data is correct and does not contradict itself across systems.
  • Protection against Modification: Preventing unauthorized alteration of data.
  • Completeness: Ensuring no data is missing or partially recorded.
  • Lifecycle Maintenance: Preserving integrity from creation to deletion of data.

How Integrity is Maintained

  • Data Validation: Using software rules to ensure only valid data is entered (e.g., proper formats, data types).
  • Access Controls: Using Role-Based Access Control (RBAC) and strict user permissions to restrict access to sensitive data, limiting accidental or malicious tampering.
  • Backup & Recovery: Regularly backing up data to ensure it can be restored if corrupted or lost.
  • Error Checking: Using checksums, cyclic redundancy checks (CRC), and hash functions (e.g., SHA-256) to detect data corruption during transfer and storage.
  • Encryption and Digital Signatures: Data is encrypted at rest and in transit to protect against unauthorized modification. Digital signatures ensure data authenticity.
  • Hardware and System Maintenance: ECC (Error-Correcting Code) RAM helps manage data corruption at the hardware level; regular system updates prevent vulnerabilities.

Components of Integrity Implementation

  • Physical Integrity: Protecting data against external factors such as natural calamities, power outages, or physical intrusion.
  • Logical Integrity: Ensures the accuracy, consistency, and validity of data within a relational database, protecting it from invalid data entry.
  • Cryptographic Verification: Using hashing (data integrity), digital signatures (authenticity & integrity), and certificates (trust) to detect unauthorized alterations.
U1·Q5

What is business ethics? Explain its key principles and importance.

Definition

Business ethics are the moral principles guiding company behavior, focusing on honesty, fairness, and accountability in all operations. It covers legal compliance, social responsibility, and ethical leadership. Key issues include environmental sustainability, fair treatment of employees, transparent reporting, and AI ethics.

Principles of Business Ethics

  1. Accountability: Being answerable for decisions and their outcomes.
  2. Respect for others: Treating employees, customers, and partners with dignity.
  3. Leadership: Ethical leaders set the tone for the entire organization.
  4. Honesty: Transparent communication with all stakeholders.
  5. Compliance with rules: Following applicable laws and regulations.
  6. Loyalty: Being faithful to the organization's values and people.
  7. Environmental concern: Minimizing ecological harm.
  8. Transparency: Open and honest operations and reporting.
  9. Integrity: Consistent alignment of values, words, and actions.
  10. Fairness: Equal and unbiased treatment of all stakeholders.

Key Examples of Business Ethics in Action

  • Customer Trust & Honesty: Voluntarily recalling a faulty product to prioritize safety over profit.
  • Environmental Sustainability: Adopting eco-friendly materials and reducing waste. (e.g., Server in Manang)
  • Fair Employment Practices: Ensuring fair wages, safe working conditions, and no discrimination.
  • Transparency & CSR: In Nepal, the Industrial Enterprise Act, 2020 mandates 1% of net profit for CSR, directed toward education, health, infrastructure, and disaster relief.
  • Data Protection: Securely handling customer data and not sharing it without permission.

Consequences of Unethical Business Practices

  • Legal penalties and fines
  • Damaged reputation and loss of customer trust
  • Decreased employee morale and productivity
  • Financial losses and potential business failure
U1·Q6

Explain the ethical decision-making framework and its steps.

Ethical Decision-Making

Ethical decision-making involves guiding choices with moral principles like integrity, fairness, and transparency to ensure accountability and respect for all stakeholders. It is especially critical in IT, where decisions affect privacy, security, and societal well-being.

Key Ethical Considerations and Principles

  • Transparency: Being open and honest about the decision-making process to build trust.
  • Fairness and Equity: Ensuring decisions treat all stakeholders justly, avoiding bias or favoritism.
  • Responsibility and Accountability: Taking ownership of the consequences of decisions on employees, customers, and the environment.
  • Respect for Rights: Upholding the dignity and rights of all individuals involved.
  • Integrity: Acting consistently with moral values and honesty.

Steps for Ethical Decision-Making

  1. Identify the problem and its ethical dimensions.
  2. Gather information and identify all stakeholders affected.
  3. Generate potential actions and consider their consequences.
  4. Evaluate alternatives based on ethical principles (fairness, harm, legality).
  5. Make a decision and implement it responsibly.
  6. Review the outcomes to learn and improve future decisions.

Key Considerations in Implementation

  • Long-Term Perspective: Prioritize sustainable, ethical outcomes over short-term gains.
  • Consider Unintended Consequences: Evaluate potential negative impacts on workers or the environment.
  • Seek Counsel: Consult with others to avoid personal bias and gain different perspectives.
U1·Q7

What are the key ethical challenges in IT? Explain with examples.

Key Ethical Challenges in IT

  • Privacy Violations: Unauthorized access to personal data (e.g., unauthorized access to patient medical records). IT systems collect vast amounts of personal data, creating risks of surveillance, tracking, and data selling.
  • Cybercrime: Hacking, phishing, and malware.
    • Phishing: Attackers trick users via email, text, or malicious websites to steal login credentials, credit card numbers, and personal data.
    • Malware: Malicious software (viruses, worms, ransomware, trojans) designed to damage, steal data, or gain unauthorized access.
  • Software Piracy: Illegal copying/distribution of software — including softlifting, end-user piracy, and internet piracy. Violates intellectual property rights and harms developers.
  • AI Bias: Algorithms can reinforce discrimination in hiring, loan approvals, and facial recognition — particularly impacting marginalized groups. Mitigation requires diverse datasets, transparency, and regular audits.
  • Data Privacy and Security: Software developers using client data without proper consent violate privacy and confidentiality principles.
  • Workplace Environment: Harassment and discrimination in tech workplaces violate principles of fairness and respect for human dignity.
  • Professional Conduct — Conflicts of Interest: Situations where personal interests conflict with professional duties (e.g., an MEC member making biased decisions). Ethics of integrity and honesty must guide such situations.
Example: Encrypting user data to prevent misuse demonstrates ethical awareness. It prevents legal issues such as lawsuits, fines, and criminal charges that arise from data breaches.

U2·Q2

Explain how IT professionals manage relationships with various stakeholders.

Managing Professional Relationships in IT

Managing professional relationships in IT means maintaining healthy, respectful, and responsible interactions with clients, employers, colleagues, and users while following ethical principles. The key ethical principles to follow are: Honesty, Confidentiality, Integrity, Respect, Accountability, and Fairness.

1. Relationship with Clients

  • How to manage: Understand requirements clearly; deliver work on time; communicate regularly; maintain transparency.
  • Ethical considerations: Do not misuse client data; do not overcharge or mislead; keep project details confidential.
  • Example: A software developer should not share a client's business data with competitors.

2. Relationship with Employers

  • How to manage: Follow company policies; be loyal and responsible; maintain professionalism.
  • Ethical considerations: Avoid conflicts of interest; do not leak company secrets; use company resources properly.
  • Example: An employee should not use office software for illegal purposes.

3. Relationship with Colleagues

  • How to manage: Work as a team; share knowledge; communicate respectfully.
  • Ethical considerations: Avoid discrimination or harassment; give credit to others' work; support fairness.
  • Example: Giving proper credit in a group project instead of claiming all the work.

4. Relationship with IT Users

  • How to manage: Provide user-friendly systems; offer support and guidance; ensure system reliability.
  • Ethical considerations: Protect user privacy; avoid system manipulation; ensure accessibility.
  • Example: A system admin should not access users' personal emails without permission.

5. Relationship with Suppliers

IT workers deal with many different hardware, software, and service providers. Good working relationships are built by dealing fairly and not making unreasonable demands. IT workers must be alert to unethical supplier behaviors — for example, offering gifts intended as bribes — and must refuse such advances.

Common Ethical Issues in Relationships

Data breaches, miscommunication, misuse of authority, plagiarism, and unfair treatment.

U2·Q3

What is a professional code of ethics? State its importance and key principles for IT workers.

Definition

A Professional Code of Ethics is a set of rules and principles that guides how IT workers and users should behave responsibly and morally while using technology. It frequently includes formal written statements about the organization's purpose, its values, and the principles that should guide employees' actions.

Purpose

It helps ensure safe use of technology, builds trust in digital systems, and protects data and privacy. It focuses employees on areas of ethical risk related to their role, provides guidance for recognizing and dealing with ethical issues, and fosters a culture of honesty and accountability.

Code of Ethics for IT Workers — Key Principles

  1. Honesty and Integrity: Be truthful in all professional work; do not provide false information. Example: A programmer should not hide software bugs from clients.
  2. Confidentiality: Protect sensitive data of clients and organizations; do not share private information. Example: A database administrator must not leak user passwords.
  3. Professional Competence: Work only within your skills and continuously improve knowledge. Example: A developer should learn new security practices to prevent hacking.
  4. Avoid Harm: Do not create harmful software (viruses, malware). Example: Writing ransomware is unethical and illegal.
  5. Respect Intellectual Property: Do not copy or steal others' work. Example: Using licensed software instead of pirated versions.
  6. Accountability: Take responsibility for your actions. Example: Fixing errors instead of blaming others.
  7. Fair Treatment: Treat all clients and colleagues equally without discrimination.

Importance of Code of Ethics

  • Builds trust in IT systems and organizations.
  • Protects users and organizations from harm.
  • Prevents cybercrime and ethical violations.
  • Promotes fairness and responsibility.
  • Enhances professional reputation.
  • Saves from unwanted penalties and legal issues.
  • Maintains dignity and respect of individuals and organizations.

U2·Q5

Explain certification and government licensing for IT professionals. What are the differences and benefits?

Certification

Certification is an official recognition that proves a person has the required skills, knowledge, and competence in a specific area of Information Technology. It is usually provided after passing an exam or completing training.

Purpose: Validate technical skills; increase job opportunities; build professional credibility; ensure quality work.

Advantages for IT Workers after Certification: Higher salary; career growth; global recognition; better job security; improved technical skills; self-satisfaction and confidence in the workplace.

Certification for IT Users: IT users (students, employees, general users) need basic IT certifications to improve digital literacy, ensure safe use of technology, increase productivity, and promote ethical use.

Government Licensing

Government Licensing is formal authorization given by a government body that allows individuals or organizations to legally perform certain IT-related activities. It ensures professionalism, accountability, and protection of users and systems.

Purpose for IT Workers: Ensures qualified professionals; maintains technical standards; protects public from fraud and cybercrime; promotes ethical behavior.

Purpose for IT Users: Ensures legal use of software; prevents software piracy; protects data privacy and security. Example: Using licensed software like Microsoft Windows or Adobe Photoshop.

In Nepal, licensing is generally administered at the Federal-Provincial-Local level. The government creates laws and policies for IT use, issues licenses, monitors compliance, and enforces penalties.

Types of Software Licenses

  • Single-user license
  • Multi-user license
  • Open-source license
  • Subscription-based license

Challenges

Rapid technology changes make licensing outdated quickly; high cost of certification; lack of global standardization; enforcement difficulties in developing countries.

U2·Q6

What are IT professional malpractices? Explain types, causes, and prevention.

Definition

IT Professional Malpractices refer to unethical, illegal, or irresponsible actions by IT professionals while performing their duties. These practices violate professional standards, laws, and codes of ethics, and can cause harm to individuals, organizations, and society.

Common Types of IT Malpractices

  • Unauthorized Access (Hacking): Breaking into systems or networks without permission.
  • Software Piracy: Illegal copying, distribution, or use of proprietary software.
  • Data Theft and Privacy Violation: Stealing or misusing personal, financial, or confidential data.
  • Spreading Malware and Viruses: Creating or distributing harmful programs.
  • Plagiarism and Intellectual Property Theft: Copying others' code, designs, or ideas without proper credit or permission.
  • Misuse of Organizational Resources: Using company systems, networks, or data for personal or unauthorized activities.
  • Falsifying Data or Reports: Manipulating system data, test results, or financial reports.

Causes of IT Malpractices

  • Lack of ethical awareness
  • Weak laws and enforcement
  • Financial greed
  • Poor organizational control
  • Pressure to achieve targets
  • Thrill-seeking or "for fun" motivation

Prevention of IT Malpractices

  • Ethical Measures: Follow professional codes such as those from ACM; cultivate an ethical organizational culture.
  • Legal Measures: Strong cyber laws and penalties; government monitoring and enforcement.
  • Technical Measures: Use strong passwords and encryption; install antivirus and firewalls.
  • Organizational Measures: Regular audits; employee training; access control systems (RBAC).
U2·Q7

What is compliance? Explain its key areas, importance, and consequences of non-compliance.

Definition

Compliance means following the rules, laws, policies, and ethical standards while using information technology systems. For IT users, it refers to the obligation to follow legal regulations, respect organizational policies, and use technology in an ethical and secure manner.

Key Areas of Compliance

  1. Software Licensing Compliance: Use only legally purchased or authorized software; avoid piracy; follow license agreements.
  2. Data Protection and Privacy: Protect personal and sensitive data; do not share confidential information without permission; follow privacy policies of platforms.
  3. Security Compliance: Use strong passwords and authentication methods; avoid suspicious links and emails; follow cybersecurity guidelines.
  4. Acceptable Use Policy (AUP): Use IT resources only for authorized purposes; avoid illegal activities; respect workplace IT rules.
  5. Intellectual Property Rights: Do not copy or distribute copyrighted content illegally; give proper credit for others' work.

Types of Compliance

  • Regulatory Compliance: Following industry-specific standards (e.g., GDPR, HIPAA) to avoid penalties.
  • Statutory Compliance: Adhering to labor laws, tax regulations, and payroll requirements.
  • Ethical Compliance: Implementing anti-fraud, anti-bribery, and anti-corruption policies.
  • Technical/Safety Compliance: Using approved technologies, following design standards and risk assessments.
  • Internal Policies: Enforcing internal Codes of Conduct to maintain workplace integrity.

Importance of Compliance

For Individuals: Protects from legal penalties; ensures safe and secure use of technology; minimizes risk of accidents and failures.
For Organizations: Prevents data breaches; maintains reputation and trust; ensures smooth operations and long-term sustainability; increases production and reduces data errors.
For Society: Reduces cybercrime; promotes ethical digital culture; ensures environmental protection in hazardous industries.

Consequences of Non-Compliance

  • Legal actions (fines or punishment)
  • Loss of access to systems
  • Data breaches and security risks
  • Damage to reputation of an individual or organization
  • Financial loss
  • Internal disruptions: increased staff turnover and management distracted by rectifying issues
  • Increased insurance and legal costs
  • Operational disruption: forced shutdowns, product recalls

Data Protection Legislation in Nepal

  • Constitution of Nepal (Article 28): Guarantees the right to privacy as a fundamental right.
  • Individual Privacy Act 2075 (2018): Safeguards the fundamental right to privacy; protects against unauthorized collection, storage, and publication of personal data.
  • Electronic Transaction Act 2063 (2008): Legalizes digital signatures, validates electronic data, and establishes regulatory bodies like the Office of Certificate Control.
  • National Civil Code 2074 (2017): Protects personal, family, property, and contract matters.

U3·Q1

What is cybercrime? Explain the types of IT security incidents and their impacts.

Definition of Cybercrime

Computer and internet crime (cybercrime) refers to illegal activities carried out using computers, networks, or the internet. These crimes can target individuals, organizations, or even governments. Examples include hacking, online fraud, identity theft, spreading malware, and phishing.

Causes of IT Security Incidents

IT security incidents happen primarily due to: human error and negligence (misconfiguring cloud storage, falling for phishing); sophisticated cyberattacks (ransomware); software vulnerabilities (unpatched software); misconfigured cloud environments; insider threats (malicious or negligent employees); and third-party/supply chain compromises.

Types of IT Security Incidents

  1. Unauthorized Access: Someone gains access to a system without permission. Example: Hacking a school database.
  2. Data Breach: Sensitive data is exposed or stolen (e.g., loss of a laptop or hard disk containing unencrypted data).
  3. Malware Attack: Unauthorized installation of malicious software — including ransomware (locking files), spyware (stealing info), trojans (disguised malware), viruses, and worms.
  4. Phishing Attack: Fake messages via email, text, or malicious websites designed to trick users into giving personal information, login credentials, or credit card numbers.
  5. Insider Threat: An authorized person misuses their access to steal data or sabotage systems.

Impact of IT Security Incidents

  • Financial loss for individuals or organizations
  • Loss of critical data
  • Damage to reputation
  • Legal consequences and regulatory penalties
  • Severe operational shutdowns, disrupting production and service delivery

Prevention Measures

  • Use strong passwords
  • Install antivirus software
  • Regular software updates
  • User awareness training
  • Use digital signatures and hash functions
U3·Q2

What are exploits? Explain the common types of exploits with examples.

Definition

An exploit is a piece of code, a sequence of commands, or a specific technique used to take advantage of a vulnerability (a bug or weakness) in a system. Exploits are designed to trigger unintended behavior in software or hardware — such as gaining unauthorized access, escalating privileges, or stealing data. An exploit is typically the delivery mechanism (it "breaks the lock") rather than the malicious payload (like ransomware or a virus).

Common Types of Exploits

1. Software Exploits (targeting bugs in software):

  • Virus: A piece of programming code disguised as something else that causes a computer to behave unexpectedly. A virus does not spread by itself — it spreads when a user opens an infected email attachment, downloads an infected program, or visits an infected website.
  • Worm: A harmful program that resides in active memory and duplicates itself. Unlike viruses, worms can propagate without human intervention — often sending copies of themselves to other computers by email.
  • Trojan Horse: A program in which malicious code is hidden inside a seemingly harmless program. It can destroy hard drives, corrupt files, control the computer remotely, steal passwords, or launch attacks against other computers. Delivered via email attachments, websites, or removable media (USB, CD).

2. Network Exploits:

  • Intercepting Wi-Fi Data: Unauthorized capture of login credentials, emails, and financial details as they travel wirelessly.
  • DDoS (Distributed Denial of Service): A malicious hacker takes control of multiple computers via the internet and causes them to flood a target site with requests, making it unavailable.
  • Man-in-the-Middle (MitM) Attack: The attacker intercepts and potentially alters communication between two parties. The attacker sits between the device and the Wi-Fi router, appearing as the router to the device and as the device to the router.

3. Web Application Exploits: Target websites and web apps. Example: SQL Injection — injecting malicious SQL code into a web form to access or manipulate a database.

4. Zero-Day Exploit: Attacks on software or hardware vulnerabilities that are not yet known to the vendor or public. Example: Exploiting a security flaw before a patch is released.

Why Exploits Are Dangerous

Hard to detect; can bypass security systems; can lead to full system control and data theft.

Protection Against Exploits

  • Keep software updated and install security patches
  • Use web application firewalls (WAF)
  • Enable automatic updates
  • Avoid suspicious links
U3·Q3

Explain the types of perpetrators in cybercrime — hackers, cybercriminals, and cyberterrorists.

Definition

A perpetrator is a person, group, or institution that commits a harmful, illegal, or immoral act. In the context of cybercrime, perpetrators exploit technology to carry out attacks, fraud, or disruptions.

Types of Perpetrators

1. Hackers (Digital):

Skilled computer users who break into systems. Not all hackers are criminals — some are ethical.

  • White-Hat Hackers (Ethical Hackers): Security professionals who use their skills legally and with permission to strengthen security. They include Penetration Testers (hired to find vulnerabilities before criminals do), Bug Bounty Hunters (independent researchers rewarded for finding security flaws), and Red Team Specialists (simulate real-world attackers to test organizational defenses).
  • Black-Hat Hackers: Illegal attackers who breach systems for personal gain, to cause damage, or to steal data — without permission.
  • Grey-Hat Hackers: Hybrid — may break into systems without permission but without malicious intent, often to expose vulnerabilities and inform the owner.

2. Cybercriminals:

Motivated primarily by monetary gain. They hack into computers to steal money (often by transferring funds across multiple accounts), steal and resell credit card numbers, personal identities, and cell phone IDs. The high potential for monetary gain means they can afford to buy technical expertise from unethical insiders.

3. Cyberterrorists:

Seek to cause harm rather than gather information. They use techniques that destroy or disrupt services, consider themselves at war, have a very high acceptance of risk, and seek maximum impact. Examples of cyberterrorist tactics: hacking into critical infrastructure, introducing viruses to vulnerable networks, website defacing, Denial-of-Service attacks, and terroristic threats via electronic communication.

Reducing Insider Threats — Organizational Steps

  • Perform thorough background checks and psychological testing for sensitive positions.
  • Carefully limit the number of people who can perform sensitive operations; grant only the minimum necessary rights (principle of least privilege).
  • Define job roles so the same person cannot both initiate and approve an action.
  • Periodically rotate employees in sensitive positions.
  • Immediately revoke all rights when someone moves to a new position.
  • Implement an ongoing audit process to review key actions and procedures.
U3·Q4

What is trustworthy computing? Explain its key pillars and aspects of implementation.

Definition

Trustworthy Computing involves adopting a comprehensive, long-term approach to software and hardware development that prioritizes security, privacy, reliability, and business integrity. It was originally introduced by Microsoft in 2002 (refined 2012) and focuses on mitigating computer crime by fostering user trust and protecting data.

Four Pillars of Trustworthy Computing

  1. Security: Proactive measures such as encryption and firewalls to protect data and infrastructure from unauthorized access. Implementations include Trusted Platform Modules (TPMs) in hardware to ensure system integrity.
  2. Privacy: Ensuring user data is collected only with consent, used only for intended purposes, and protected from misuse.
  3. Reliability: Systems must be dependable and available when needed. Regularly updating operating systems and software to patch vulnerabilities.
  4. Business Integrity: Honest, transparent, and ethical business operations and data handling.

Key Aspects of Implementation

  • Security Pillars: Using encryption, firewalls, and TPMs to protect against malware and phishing by ensuring system integrity.
  • Reliability and Maintenance: Regular software updates to patch vulnerabilities exploited by cybercriminals.
  • Education and Policy: Training employees to recognize phishing, managing identity theft risks, and implementing strong password policies.
U3·Q5

What is a security policy? Explain its objectives and steps to establish one.

Definition

A security policy is a formal document that defines how information and systems should be protected, what users can and cannot do, and how to respond to cyber incidents. It acts as a framework for preventing computer and internet crimes.

Objectives of a Security Policy

  • Protect confidential data
  • Ensure integrity (data is not altered improperly)
  • Maintain availability (systems are accessible when needed)
  • Prevent unauthorized access and cybercrime
  • Ensure legal and ethical use of IT resources
  • Risk mitigation across the organization
  • Consistency: Provide uniform rules for the entire organization so security is handled uniformly across departments.
  • Accountability: Define clear roles and responsibilities so everyone knows expectations and who is responsible if a breach occurs.

Steps to Establish a Security Policy

  1. Step 1 — Identify Needs: Understand the organization's assets and risks.
  2. Step 2 — Define Objectives: Set clear security goals aligned with organizational values.
  3. Step 3 — Draft the Policy: Write rules, procedures, and guidelines.
  4. Step 4 — Review and Approve: Get approval from management and legal teams.
  5. Step 5 — Implement the Policy: Apply security measures (tools, software, access controls).
  6. Step 6 — Educate Users: Train employees about security practices through workshops and awareness campaigns.
  7. Step 7 — Monitor and Update: Regularly review and improve the policy to address new threats.

Challenges in Implementation

Lack of awareness; resistance from users; rapidly changing technology; cost of security tools; inadequate infrastructure; lack of awareness; inadequate legal and weak regulatory framework.

U3·Q6

Explain risk assessment in the context of computer and internet crime.

Risk Assessment — Key Components

  1. Asset Identification: Documenting all digital assets, including hardware, software, and data.
  2. Threat Identification: Identifying potential threats such as hackers, malware, phishing, insider threats, and natural disasters.
  3. Vulnerability Assessment: Finding weaknesses using tools like scanners and penetration tests. (e.g., weak passwords, outdated software, lack of security policies)
  4. Risk Analysis (Impact and Likelihood): Evaluating the probability of a risk occurring and the severity of its consequences, often using a risk matrix to prioritize threats.
  5. Risk Evaluation: Prioritizing risks based on severity to determine which actions require immediate attention.
  6. Risk Treatment: Implementing controls to mitigate risks — such as multi-factor authentication or employee training.

Types of Risks

  • Security Risks: Unauthorized access, malware, data breaches.
  • Operational Risks: System failures, loss of data, network downtime.
  • Legal Risks: Violation of cyber laws, copyright infringement, privacy issues.
  • Financial Risks: Online fraud, system damage, business interruption.
  • Reputational Risks: Loss of customer trust, damage to brand image.

Risk Assessment Methods

  • Qualitative: Using subjective, descriptive scales (low/medium/high) based on expert judgment and experience. Useful for quick assessment in early project stages.
  • Quantitative: Using numerical data (e.g., financial impact) for more precise risk determination.
  • Failure Modes and Effects Analysis (FMEA): A proactive, structured method to identify, prioritize, and mitigate potential failures.
  • Fault Tree Analysis (FTA): A top-down approach analyzing what causes a system to fail, using logical AND/OR gates to visualize failure paths.

Best Practices for Effective Risk Management

  • Conduct regular risk assessments, not just once.
  • Prioritize the most significant risks first.
  • Develop a Risk Treatment Plan addressing identified risks.
  • Create an Assurance Plan to verify effectiveness of the assessment.
U3·Q7

How should organizations educate employees to prevent cybercrime? Explain prevention, detection, and response.

Why Employee Education is Important

Many cybercrimes (phishing, hacking) target people, not just systems. Employees may unknowingly expose data; contract workers often have limited supervision but still have access to systems. Awareness reduces human errors and insider threats significantly.

Methods of Educating Employees

  • Training Programs and Workshops: Interactive workshops including live hacking demos, role-playing, and hands-on scenarios — moving beyond static presentations.
  • Cybersecurity Awareness Campaigns: Ongoing, not one-time, campaigns to keep security top-of-mind.
  • Regular Updates and Reminders: Monthly updates on evolving threats; immediate reminders after security incidents.
  • Simulated Phishing Tests: Regular phishing simulations (e.g., monthly) to train employees to recognize malicious emails.
  • Written Security Policies: Clear, accessible, up-to-date policies on password management, BYOD (Bring Your Own Device) usage, and incident reporting — signed annually by all employees.

Prevention — Stopping Cybercrime Before It Happens

  • Strong Password Practices: Use complex passwords and change them regularly.
  • Phishing Awareness: Do not click suspicious links or emails.
  • Safe Internet Use: Avoid unsafe websites and downloads.
  • Device Security: Lock computers and use antivirus software.
  • Data Protection: Do not share confidential information.

Detection — Identifying Cyber Threats Early

  • Recognize unusual system behavior
  • Identify suspicious emails or attachments
  • Notice unauthorized access attempts
  • Detect slow systems or unknown software installations

Response — What to Do After a Cyber Incident

Immediate Actions: Report incident to IT/security team; disconnect affected device from network; do not attempt to fix alone.

Organizational Response: Investigate the issue; recover lost data from backups; fix vulnerabilities; inform affected users if needed.

U4·Q1

Define privacy. Explain its importance and types. Discuss privacy laws in Nepal.

Definition of Privacy

Privacy is the policy and preference that dictates who is allowed to see your data, what data should be collected in the first place, and gives you the right to consent. It is the right of an individual to control their personal information, while anonymity ensures an individual's identity remains completely unknown.

Types of Privacy

  • Physical Privacy: The ability to be alone, out of public sight, and free from physical intrusion or observation in personal spaces.
  • Data (Information) Privacy: The right to control how personal details — such as medical records, finances, and digital footprint — are collected, stored, and shared.
  • Decisional Privacy: The freedom to make personal choices about your life, body, and relationships without government or societal interference.

Importance of Privacy

  • Protects individual freedom
  • Prevents misuse of personal information
  • Reduces identity theft risks
  • Builds trust in organizations
  • Encourages safe online communication
  • Supports human dignity

Information Privacy Challenges

  • Transparency: Organizations must clearly explain what data they collect and why.
  • Lawful Use: Personal data should only be used for the specific purpose for which it was originally gathered.
  • Security: Appropriate technical and organizational measures must be in place to safeguard personal data.

Privacy Laws in Nepal

Constitution of Nepal (2072), Article 28: Guarantees the right to privacy as a fundamental right — personal life, residence, and data are inviolable.

Individual Privacy Act 2075 (2018): The primary legislation regulating protection of personal information. Section 3 ensures confidentiality of physical/mental condition; Section 4 protects family life. Penalties: Imprisonment up to 3 years, fines up to NPR 30,000, or both.

Electronic Transaction Act 2063 (2008): Regulates cybercrimes, data protection, and digital signatures; legalizes digital signatures and validates electronic data.

National Civil Code 2074 (2017): Section 21 prohibits unauthorized entry into a person's residence, recording conversations, publishing personal activities, taking photos, or imitating a person's identity without consent.

National Criminal Code 2074 (2017): Criminalizes listening to/recording others' conversations (Section 293), exposing confidential matter (Section 294), taking photographs without consent (Section 295).

Proposed IT Bill (2024): Aims to increase fines for privacy violations to NPR 500,000 and jail up to 5 years.

Punishments for Cybercrimes (ETA)

  • Unauthorized access & system damage (hacking): Up to 3 years imprisonment or fine up to NPR 200,000, or both.
  • Publication of illegal/defamatory materials: Up to 5 years in prison or fine up to NPR 100,000, or both.
  • Computer fraud & forgery: Up to 2 years in prison or fine up to NPR 100,000, or both.
  • Breach of confidentiality: Up to 2 years in prison or fine up to NPR 100,000, or both.
U4·Q2

What is GDPR? Explain its key features and benefits.

Definition

The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) is a European privacy law that establishes strict standards for how organizations handle personal data, emphasizing accountability, transparency, and protection of user privacy across all digital interactions. It protects personal data of EU/EEA citizens and applies to any organization worldwide that offers goods/services to or monitors the behavior of EU residents.

Nepal does not have a single comprehensive data protection law like GDPR. Its official equivalent is the Privacy Act, 2075 (2018).

Key Features of GDPR

  • Legality, Fairness, and Transparency: Data must be collected on a legal basis (explicit consent) and processed openly.
  • Purpose Limitation: Data can only be collected for specified, explicit, and legitimate purposes; cannot be repurposed.
  • Data Minimization: Only the minimum necessary data for the intended purpose should be collected.
  • Accuracy: Personal data must be kept accurate and up to date.
  • Storage Limitation: Data must not be kept longer than necessary.
  • Integrity and Confidentiality: Data must be secured against unauthorized access, loss, or damage using encryption and protective measures.
  • Accountability: Organizations must be able to demonstrate compliance with all GDPR principles.

Core Rights Under GDPR

Individuals hold the right to: access, correct, delete ("right to be forgotten"), and transfer their data; object to automated profiling. Legal conditions such as explicit consent or documented legitimate interest must be satisfied for processing personal data.

Penalties

Heavy fines for non-compliance: up to €20 million or 4% of a company's global revenue, whichever is higher. Organizations must also report data breaches.

Benefits of GDPR

  • Improves customer trust within organizations
  • Enhances data security
  • Promotes transparency in data handling
  • Encourages responsible data handling
  • Reduces misuse of personal information
  • Increases accountability of organizations
U4·Q3

What are data breaches? Explain their causes and how to prevent them.

Definition

A data breach is a security incident in which unauthorized individuals gain access to view, steal, or expose confidential and sensitive information. Data breaches expose personal information, damage organizational reputation, cause financial losses, and require immediate response.

Causes of Data Breaches

  • Human Errors: Unintentional mistakes by employees, such as sending sensitive emails to the wrong recipient or misconfiguring cloud databases.
  • Malware Attacks: Cybercriminals exploiting system vulnerabilities, using stolen credentials, or launching ransomware attacks.
  • Insider Threats: Malicious or negligent employees intentionally or accidentally exposing data.
  • Unsecured Networks: Connecting to open or inadequately secured Wi-Fi networks.
  • Weak Passwords: Easy-to-guess or reused passwords that allow unauthorized access.
  • Poor Security Systems: Outdated antivirus, lack of firewalls, or absence of data encryption.
  • Physical Theft: Loss or theft of hardware like laptops, physical documents, or USB drives containing unencrypted data.

Preventing Data Breaches

  • Use strong, unique passwords for all accounts
  • Enable multi-factor authentication (MFA)
  • Encrypt sensitive information at rest and in transit
  • Update software and apply security patches regularly
  • Train employees on cybersecurity best practices
  • Monitor and log suspicious activities continuously
Electronic Discovery (E-Discovery) is the legal process of identifying, preserving, collecting, and producing Electronically Stored Information (ESI) for lawsuits or investigations. In case of a data breach leading to legal action, organizations must follow the E-Discovery lifecycle: Identification → Preservation → Collection → Processing → Review → Production.
U4·Q4

What is consumer profiling and workplace monitoring? Discuss their ethical issues.

Consumer Profiling

Consumer profiling is the creation of a detailed, data-driven description of an ideal customer or target audience. It compiles demographic, psychographic, and behavioral data to help businesses understand exactly who they are selling to, allowing for tailored marketing and personalized experiences.

Data analyzed in consumer profiling:

  • Demographics: Age, gender, income, education level, and occupation.
  • Psychographics: Values, beliefs, interests, personality traits, and lifestyle.
  • Behavioral Data: Purchasing habits, brand loyalty, spending triggers, and interaction with products.
  • Geography: Physical location, climate, and regional preferences.

Why consumer profiles are needed: Targeted marketing (spending budgets efficiently); product development (aligning offerings with customer needs); improved retention (personalizing customer journeys to build loyalty).

Ethical Issues: Consent — users may not know their data is being collected; data privacy — profiling aggregates sensitive personal data; discriminatory targeting — certain groups may be excluded or exploited; data misuse — profiles may be sold to third parties without user knowledge.

Workplace Monitoring

Workplace monitoring means the tracking of employee activity, communications, or location using software, devices, or physical surveillance. It includes email and internet usage monitoring, helps improve productivity, prevents misuse of resources, but may affect employee privacy.

Common Methods:

  • Digital Tracking: Monitoring work emails, keystrokes, application usage, and browser histories.
  • Physical Surveillance: CCTV cameras, badge-entry systems, and GPS trackers on company vehicles.
  • Communication Recording: Listening to or recording business phone calls and virtual meetings.

Ethical Issues: Invasion of privacy — employees may feel their personal space is violated; loss of trust — excessive monitoring reduces employee morale; data retention — recordings and logs may be misused; transparency — employees must be informed about monitoring policies.

Balance: Monitoring must be proportionate, transparent, and conducted with clear policies. Employees should be informed about what is monitored, why, and how the data will be used.

Advanced Surveillance Technology

Includes CCTV and facial recognition; used for security purposes; collects large amounts of data; raises significant privacy concerns; governments use surveillance systems; ethical balance between security and privacy is essential.

U4·Q5

Explain privacy and bias issues in AI/Machine Learning. How can they be mitigated?

Privacy Issues in AI/ML

AI models learn from data, and if that data contains historical inequalities or sensitive personal information, the resulting systems can replicate those biases and unintentionally compromise individual privacy.

  • Data Memorization: AI models can sometimes "memorize" sensitive pieces of information (names, medical histories, social security numbers) from their training data, making it possible to extract this data from the model.
  • Membership Inference Attacks (MIA): Adversaries can analyze a trained model's outputs to determine whether a specific individual's data was used during training, violating privacy.
  • AI systems can affect hiring decisions, impact loan approvals, and influence policing systems — all with major ethical consequences.

Types of Algorithmic/AI Bias

Type of Bias Description Example
Data Bias Training data contains discrimination (e.g., gender bias in past hiring records); the algorithm amplifies it Overrepresentation of certain demographics in diagnostic datasets
Representation Bias Training data lacks diversity, causing AI to fail or perform poorly for underrepresented groups Facial recognition misidentifying individuals with darker skin tones
Measurement/Proxy Bias Using inappropriate proxy variables (e.g., zip code as strict proxy for creditworthiness) penalizes specific demographics Variations in test sensitivity across demographic groups
Sampling Bias Bias from non-random sampling methods in data collection, leading to skewed conclusions Insufficient dissection methods in pathology data
Algorithmic Bias Bias introduced during algorithm design itself, affecting outcomes Skewed prioritization of symptoms in diagnostic algorithms

Mitigation Strategies & Solutions

  • Privacy-Preserving Machine Learning (PPML): Allows developers to train models on diverse, real-world data without exposing sensitive personal information.
  • Federated Learning: Enables AI models to train across decentralized devices or servers (like smartphones) without centralizing raw, sensitive data.
  • Differential Privacy: Adds calculated "noise" to data so queries reveal overall trends without exposing individual contributions.
  • Fairness Audits: Regularly testing AI outputs across different demographic groups to detect and neutralize biases before systems go into production.
  • Diverse Development Teams: Building inclusive AI teams ensures multiple perspectives are represented, key to catching blind spots and implicit biases during algorithmic design.

Ethical Concerns in AI

  • Lack of accountability in automated decisions
  • Data misuse risks (social media habits, medical info, political views)
  • Loss of privacy through data aggregation
  • Automated decision-making problems (no human oversight)
  • Security vulnerabilities (software bugs, misconfigured cloud storage)
  • Need for responsible AI governance and regulation
U4·Q6

What is differential privacy? Explain its advantages with real-world examples.

Definition

Differential Privacy is a mathematical technique where the goal is to extract useful information from a dataset without compromising the privacy of the individuals in it. It works by adding calculated "noise" (random statistical perturbation) to data so that queries about a dataset reveal overall trends without exposing individual contributions.

Advantages of Differential Privacy

  • Shares data without revealing individual identity
  • Reduces risks of data leakage
  • Useful in research, innovation, and statistics
  • Balances privacy and usefulness of data
  • Flexible and adaptable — can be applied to various types of data, algorithms, and scenarios
  • Enhances trust in data systems
  • Reduces re-identification risks

Real-World Examples

  • Apple: Uses differential privacy to collect and analyze data from users' devices — keyboard usage, emoji preferences, web browsing patterns, and health metrics — while protecting their privacy and identity. Apple claims it does not see or store raw data, but only aggregates noisy data to improve Siri, Safari, and HealthKit.
  • Google: Uses differential privacy to collect and analyze data from users' web and app activity — Chrome usage, YouTube views, Maps searches — while protecting privacy. Google claims it does not link the noisy data with other data but uses it to improve Chrome, YouTube, and Maps.
  • Microsoft: Uses differential privacy to collect and analyze data from customers' devices — Windows usage, Office productivity, Xbox gaming — while protecting privacy and security. Microsoft uses the noisy data to improve Windows, Office, and Xbox products.
U4·Q7

What is Nepal's Cyber Security Policy 2080? Explain its objectives and challenges.

Nepal's National Cyber Security Policy (2080/2023)

The National Cyber Security Policy (2080/2023) serves as Nepal's foundational framework for protecting citizens, businesses, and government infrastructure from digital threats.

Key Objectives

  • Infrastructure Protection: Ensuring the resilience, integrity, and security of critical ICT infrastructure across both public and private sectors.
  • Risk Management: Mitigating cyber threats, vulnerabilities, and attacks through structured, real-time threat intelligence and information sharing.
  • Incident Response: Establishing standardized emergency response protocols and strengthening institutions like the National Information Technology Center (NITC) of Nepal.
  • Data Privacy: Setting baseline protocols for data protection to safeguard citizen privacy against unauthorized access.
  • International Coordination: Exchanging bilateral, regional, and international coordination and knowledge sharing to reduce cybersecurity risks.

Problems Related to Cyber Security in Nepal

  • Lack of effective legal provisions and institutional structures for cybersecurity.
  • Lack of physical and technological infrastructure related to cybersecurity.
  • Shortage of skilled human resources and research in the cybersecurity sector.
  • Lack of awareness related to cybersecurity among general public and organizations.
  • Lack of internal and external coordination in cybersecurity.

Key Challenges

  • Managing policy-level and structural measures to reduce cyberattack risks on ICT systems.
  • Developing and utilizing skilled manpower through timely research to ensure cybersecurity.
  • Identifying and protecting National Critical Infrastructure.
  • Controlling unauthorized access to public, commercial, and personal data.
  • Ensuring reliable digital systems and cybersecurity in citizen services.
  • Cooperating and coordinating nationally and internationally for cybersecurity.

Data Protection Regulatory Bodies in Nepal

  • Ministry of Communication and Information Technology (MoCIT): Oversees broader national policies regarding digital governance and data protection.
  • National Information Commission (NIC): Regulates the Right to Information (RTI) and monitors public bodies.
  • Nepal Telecommunications Authority (NTA): Acts as the regulatory body for telecommunications and enforces privacy policies within the telecom and ISP sector.
Note: Nepal currently lacks a centralized, independent Data Protection Authority (DPA) to actively monitor compliance or enforce fines across corporate and public sectors — a key gap in the country's privacy protection framework.
